# **Key facts**

- Adults should aim for at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity physical activity per week, per WHO guidance.
- Regular physical activity reduces the risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes and certain cancers, and improves mental health, cognitive function and sleep.
- Globally, about 31% of adults and roughly 80% of adolescents do not meet recommended activity levels.
- People who are insufficiently active have a 20–30% higher risk of death compared with people who are sufficiently active.
- For most people, total daily energy and nutrient intake matters far more than precisely timing meals around a workout – there is no single correct pre/post-exercise eating window that applies universally.

---

# **Overview**

Physical activity and nutrition are closely linked, but the two are often over-simplified into rigid "eat this many minutes before/after exercise" rules. The WHO's physical activity guidance itself focuses on how much activity to get and its health benefits, rather than prescribing specific nutrition timing – for most people, meeting overall daily calorie and protein needs consistently matters more than exact workout-adjacent timing.

# **WHO guidance on physical activity**

### **Recommended activity levels**

Adults should get at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity (or an equivalent amount of vigorous-intensity activity) per week. Activity can be accumulated throughout the week in whatever pattern fits a person's schedule.

### **Health benefits**

For adults, regular activity is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, hypertension, type 2 diabetes and certain cancers, along with improved mental health, cognitive function and sleep quality. For children and adolescents, activity supports bone health, muscle development and cardiometabolic health.

# **General nutrition timing around exercise**

### **Total intake matters more than exact timing**

For most people exercising at a recreational or moderate level, consistently meeting overall daily calorie and protein needs has a bigger effect on performance and recovery than precisely timing meals relative to a workout. Eating a combination of carbohydrate and protein within a few hours of exercise (before or after) can support performance and recovery, but there is no single "correct" window that applies to everyone.

### **Hydration**

Adequate fluid intake before, during and after exercise supports performance, particularly for longer or more intense sessions (see the separate hydration knowledge doc for general fluid-intake guidance).

### **When more precise timing matters**

Endurance athletes and people training at high volume or intensity may benefit from more deliberate fueling strategies around specific sessions. This is a more specialized, sport-specific consideration and falls outside general public-health activity guidance – individualized advice from a sports dietitian or coach is more appropriate at that level.
