# **Key facts**

- Healthy eating is a cornerstone of diabetes management – it helps keep blood sugar within target range alongside medication and physical activity.
- There is no single "diabetes diet" that works for everyone; effective approaches are individualized and generally build on the same core principles as healthy eating for anyone.
- Carbohydrate counting and the plate method are two common practical tools for managing the amount and timing of carbohydrate intake.
- Regular physical activity – a commonly cited general target is around 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week – is recommended alongside dietary management.
- Specific, individualized diabetes dietary guidance should come from a healthcare professional or registered dietitian, not general information alone.

---

# **Overview**

Managing blood sugar is central to living well with diabetes, and what a person eats – along with how much and when – has a direct effect on blood sugar levels. Rather than a restrictive or highly specific meal plan, current guidance emphasizes eating healthy foods in appropriate amounts at fairly consistent times, choosing minimally processed foods, and pairing dietary choices with regular physical activity. Because diabetes management varies by individual (type of diabetes, medications, other health conditions), general information should support, not replace, guidance from a healthcare professional.

# **CDC guidance on eating well with diabetes**

### **Carbohydrate counting**

Carbohydrates have the largest direct effect on blood sugar of the three macronutrients. Tracking the grams of carbohydrate eaten at each meal is one practical way to help keep blood sugar in a target range, particularly for people using insulin who need to match doses to carbohydrate intake.

### **The plate method**

A simpler alternative to precise carb counting: fill about half of the plate with non-starchy vegetables, one quarter with a lean protein source, and one quarter with carbohydrate-containing foods (whole grains, starchy vegetables, fruit, or dairy). This provides a visual, portion-based way to build balanced meals without counting every gram.

### **Meal timing and consistency**

Eating meals at fairly consistent times, and avoiding skipping meals, helps keep blood sugar more stable over the course of a day. This matters more for people on certain medications (e.g. insulin) than for general healthy eating.

### **Physical activity's role**

Physical activity is considered a foundation of diabetes management alongside diet – it helps the body use insulin more effectively and lowers cardiovascular risk. A commonly cited general target is about 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week.

### **When to seek professional guidance**

General nutrition information should not be treated as a substitute for medical advice. Because diabetes type, medications, and individual health circumstances vary significantly, a healthcare professional or registered dietitian is best placed to give a specific, individualized eating plan.
